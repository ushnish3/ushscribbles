---
title: "When Fixes Backfire: The Story of the Cobra Effect" date: "2026-07-26"
---
When we’re offered monetary incentives to solve a widely critical problem, we seek to stretch the incentive as much as we can, often making the problems worse. 

This strange turn of events is known as the Cobra effect. It’s an economic concept brought by German scientist Horst Siebert in 2001, but the idea originates from a famous story in colonial India. Myth or truth, I can’t be the judge. 

During colonial rule in India, government officials dangled cash payouts for dead cobras to purge the streets.

Cobra turn-ins started slow, but collections soon surged as citizens flocked to grab the rewards.

Within months, opportunistic residents exploited the system by farming cobras in secret and recruiting others to do the same.

When officials scrapped the scheme, breeders dumped the worthless snakes, flooding the cities with far more wild cobras than existed before.

At the heart of this issue is how humans react to incentives. When leaders try to manage broad systems, they cannot watch everything that happens. 

To save time, they set simple numerical targets, such as counting items turned in or goals met. 

However, people quickly figure out the easiest way to hit those target numbers without actually doing the intended work. 

Economists call this Goodhart's Law: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

When a cash reward or a job evaluation depends on a single simple number, doing the right thing takes a back seat to playing the game.

A real-world example occurred in 1902, when French rulers in Hanoi, Vietnam, tried to stop an outbreak of bubonic plague. 

To eliminate rats, the government offered a small cash reward for every rat tail turned in. 

People caught rats, chopped off their tails for money, and released the live animals back into the sewer system so they could breed more rats with tails for future payouts. 

Inspectors even found rat-breeding farms running on the edges of the city. The plan failed, and the rat population boomed.

The cobra effect is everywhere, from banking scandals to environmental policies. When managers rely on rigid targets without watching how people react, they invite trickery and waste. 

True success comes from setting balanced goals, testing rules for loopholes before starting them, and rewarding honest, long-term effort rather than quick short-term targets.

Here are a few more key facts for you to dive into: 

### **The Basics**

* **The Core Idea:** The Cobra Effect happens when a rule or reward meant to fix a problem actually makes the problem worse.  
* **Why It Happens:** People naturally adjust their behavior to earn rewards or avoid punishments, often finding shortcuts that defeat the main goal.  
* **Where It Shows Up:** This issue is common in government programs, business quotas, environmental policy, and healthcare.

### **Key People and Ideas**

* **Horst Siebert:** The German economist who brought the term into modern economic discussions in 2001\.  
* **Goodhart's Law:** A rule stating that when a simple number becomes a target, it stops being a good way to measure success.  
* **Information Gap:** Leaders rarely see everything happening on the ground, making it easy for workers or citizens to trick the measurement system.

### **Key Events and Real-World Examples**

* **Delhi Cobras (1800s):** A popular story claims British leaders offered cash for dead cobras, leading locals to raise snakes for money. While snake-farming was likely just a rumor, official bounty programs still cost huge sums without lowering snake numbers.  
* **Hanoi Rats (1902):** French leaders in Vietnam paid people for rat tails to stop the plague. Instead of exterminating rats, residents cut off tails and set live rats free to breed more.  
* **UN Carbon Credits (2000s):** Factories in Asia were paid to destroy a dangerous waste gas. To collect more cash, plants produced extra waste gas on purpose just to destroy it and claim billions in credits.  
* **Wells Fargo (2010s):** Strict sales goals pushed bank employees to open millions of fake customer accounts without permission.

### **Results and Impact**

* **Wasted Resources:** Millions of dollars were spent on rewards that created artificial industries.  
* **Worse Problems:** Canceled bounty programs often left cities with more pests or pollution than before the rewards started.  
* **Lost Visibility:** Corrupted data leaves leaders blind to actual progress and real-world conditions.

### **Solutions**

* **Balanced Goals:** Use multiple checks instead of relying on a single target number.  
* **Early Testing:** Test new policies in advance to find and fix ways people might cheat.  
* **Better Rewards:** Reward honest, long-term results rather than strict short-term quotas.
